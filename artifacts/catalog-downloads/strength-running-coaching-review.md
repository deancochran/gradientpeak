# Strength and running recovery review

## Practical defaults, not a universal optimum

For muscle-building compound sets, use clean repetitions with roughly 1–3 reps in reserve rather than mandatory failure. This catalog uses RPE 7–8 for its upper-body compound sets: about 3–2 clean reps left. Lower-body heavy squats now use RPE 8 rather than 9. Adjust load to meet the prescribed reps and effort; add load gradually when all sets are consistently easier than prescribed.

Rest is there to preserve useful performance, not to maximize breathlessness. Two minutes is a practical starting point for the upper-body compounds and higher-repetition lower-body compounds; the heavier five-rep work retains or receives three minutes. Extend recovery when technique, breathing, or performance has not recovered. These are coaching defaults, not evidence that exactly two or three minutes maximizes everyone's hypertrophy.

The dedicated power session is different: explosive intent, a moderate RPE 6 effort ceiling, no grinding, and three-minute rests. Movement-capacity, single-leg-control, trunk, mobility, and circuit sessions retain their distinct purposes; they have not all been relabeled as hypertrophy training.

## Running review

- Threshold and tempo work: recover with an easy conversational jog, not another hard effort. Existing timings retained (including 170 seconds after 510-second Cruise Tempo repetitions).
- Speed Intervals: retain the two-minute recovery after three-minute efforts; jog very easily, with a brief walk if needed to regain controlled breathing.
- Strides: retain 90 seconds after 20-second efforts, walking or jogging very easily; lengthen recovery if breathing/form has not settled.
- Hill surges: retain 125 seconds after 45-second efforts, with a gentle walk/jog downhill; do not rush an unsafe descent.
- Distance-based track recoveries stay active walking/jogging; no distance step was converted to timed passive rest.
- Norwegian protocols retain their explicitly active recoveries and original targets/timing.
- Short-recovery heart rate lags the effort change. Updated treadmill cues make clear that the recovery target is not a reason to accelerate while heart rate is settling.

## Evidence and limitations

- Singer et al. (2024), systematic review/meta-analysis: hypertrophy occurs across rest durations; a small advantage favors longer over very short rests, while additional benefit beyond 90 seconds remains uncertain. Longer practical rests can still help preserve repetition performance. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11349676/
- Helms et al. (2016), application of RIR-based RPE: distinguishes proximity to failure in lifting from generic endurance effort ratings. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4961270/
- McMillan training guide: recovery differs by session purpose; tempo/threshold jog recovery, longer recovery for speed/stride quality. This is coaching guidance, not a universal interval formula. https://www.mcmillanrunning.com/training-plan-guide/

Hypertrophy depends on progressive training volume, load, nutrition and recovery over time—not rest duration or RPE alone. These are general templates, not individualized coaching or rehabilitation prescriptions. No maximal tests or failure sets were added.
